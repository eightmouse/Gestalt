![](/media/records/maple-story/1794630108058-screenshot-2026-11-13-232729.png)

I'm not using the picture as 'SEE I'M RIGHT', I don't know this person, they might've just been joking of course or baiting, I just took a screenshot cause I thought it was funny.

I know that classic is all about edgy humor, weird stuff etc etc.. however it's not the early 2000s anymore so it's hard to distinguish someone that has same humor from a disguised hateful person, pretending to be 'just joking'.
I also did unironically met people with the sentiment of 'Koreans, Europeans and everyone outside the USA should not be allowed in our game, fuck them'. Wild.

This connects to something I've been thinking about for a while now. Even if we brought back Classic servers for every game we grew up loving, **it would never truly be the same.**

We could recreate the games perfectly. The same graphics, the same mechanics, the same maps, the same music, even the same bugs. Everything could be exactly as we remember it.

But there's one thing we can never recreate: **the people, the environment, and the time we lived in. Us. You and me included.**

I think a lot of people who grew up playing games like World of Warcraft or MapleStory underestimate just how different the internet of the early 2000s was compared to the internet of 2026.

Back then, online gaming was still a relatively niche hobby. The communities were smaller, the internet was less accessible, and the people you encountered often shared a fairly specific set of interests. That doesn't mean there weren't arguments, politics, or unpleasant people. Of course there were. But the overall environment was fundamentally different.

And more importantly, **we were different.**

Many of the people who now complain that their favorite games have been "ruined by X people" were once just kids or teenagers logging in after school, completely clueless about the world around them. They weren't thinking about politics, ideology, or the cultural identity of the people they were playing with. They were thinking about leveling up, making friends, exploring, and having fun.

Others were young adults, or even fully grown adults, but they too were experiencing those games in a completely different social and cultural environment.

Fast-forward twenty years, and everything has changed.

The internet is no longer some relatively isolated corner of society. It's an extension of everyday life, accessible to practically everyone. Political discussions, social issues, and cultural disagreements are much more visible, even among younger generations. People bring their identities, beliefs, and worldviews into online spaces, including video games.

And that's not inherently a bad thing. It's simply a reflection of how society and the internet have evolved.

But here's what I find interesting: **the very same people who complain that their childhood games aren't what they used to be are often overlooking the fact that they aren't the same people they used to be, either.**

That kid who logged into MapleStory without a care in the world is now an adult with responsibilities, opinions, political beliefs, and twenty years of experiences shaping how they perceive everything around them.

Of course the game feels different. Of course the community feels different. How could it not?

You can recreate a game from 2005, but you cannot recreate the society of 2005. You cannot recreate the internet of 2005. And you certainly cannot turn everyone playing it back into the people they were twenty years ago.

That doesn't mean every change is necessarily good, or that people aren't allowed to dislike the direction a game or its community has taken. There are perfectly legitimate criticisms to be made.

But I think there's a difference between criticizing something that has genuinely changed for the worse and expecting an entire community to remain frozen in time just because that's how you remember it.

Some of us can accept that things change. We can embrace the new environment, enjoy the game for what it is today, revisit a Classic version for nostalgia, or simply move on if it no longer appeals to us.

And then there are those who return to Classic, discover that the people and communities aren't exactly how they remember them, and start complaining that even Classic has been "ruined by X people" or whatever other reason they can find.

At that point, I have to wonder whether the problem was ever really the game in the first place.

**Because sometimes, what people desperately want back isn't the old game. It's the old world they experienced it in, and the person they were when they first played it.**

And unfortunately, no Classic server can give you that.

Yet here we are, twenty years later, with people still blaming everyone else for that feeling of something being lost.

And people like me, having to listen to it and apparently writing entire notes about it.

:::note 13 / 11 / 2026 - Winter Update
# A Classic mess
The update is finally out, I was quite excited months ago when I saw all the QoL but also worried cause Nexon hates GMS and they always find a way to screw people over or ruin events.

They did it again.

The update has QoL, lots of events and a new class. Let's start with the QoL:
- Bossing improvements (20mins boss fights, boss hp reduction)
- Legion system overhaul
- Equipment Auto-Enhancement
- Daily and weekly content improvements, this one is huge.
- Inventory, equipment, and interface improvements
And lots more stuff.

Although these changes are more than welcome and needed, issues stem from Nexon implementations of even alongside these type of QoL. On one hand, we get these changes that make the game 100x better.. on the other hand, GMS gets screwed AGAIN through events.
Enough being vague.

## Quanah,  Lethe & Challenge World
The most frustrating part is that Nexon is simultaneously promoting three major features:
- Lethe: A brand-new class with exclusive launch rewards.
- Challenger World: A fresh progression environment with substantial catch-up events.
- Quanah: A special progression world with exclusive rewards, including a potentialable badge.
Yet choosing to participate in Quanah can prevent the designated character from accessing much of the event content that would ordinarily help it grow.

The New Recruit restriction is particularly questionable. The event advertises additional rewards for newly created Lethe characters, but players cannot even access it.

## Why I think this is poor event design
I can understand Nexon's intention to prevent players from stacking multiple progression systems and gaining an unintended advantage in Quanah.

But there's a substantial difference between preventing an exploit and making a player's character ineligible for unrelated launch rewards.

Lethe is the clearest example. Her exclusive New Recruit rewards are designed to celebrate the class's release. Those rewards shouldn't necessarily be tied to whether that character eventually enters Quanah.

The decision is also confusing because the restrictions apply when a character is designated for Quanah, not necessarily only after entering Quanah itself. Players therefore encounter the consequences while they're still leveling in Challenger World.

## What Nexon could have done differently
I think three relatively straightforward changes would have addressed most of this frustration:
- Allow Quanah-designated Lethe characters to complete the New Recruit Support Mission and receive their exclusive cosmetics.
- Make Quanah designation reversible until the character actually transfers worlds.
- Provide an explicit eligibility comparison before players commit, showing exactly which events and rewards they sacrifice.

The frustrating irony is that MapleStory has just received numerous excellent changes that reduce unnecessary friction, while the winter event structure introduces an entirely new kind of friction.

Solid update, terrible execution.. as always.
